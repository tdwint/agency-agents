# How To Be A Blessing

A young man named Brian grew up in a Marine household. His father was a career officer. Discipline was the language of that home. Words of love or tenderness were not spoken.

Brian enlisted after high school. His father's happiest day. But it didn't last. After weeks of attitude problems and a fight with his drill instructor, Brian was dishonorably discharged.

That ended things between them. No contact for years.

During those years Brian struggled. He was smart — above average intelligence — but he worked jobs far below his ability. He got engaged three times and broke it off every time, weeks before the wedding. He couldn't believe someone could actually love him.

Then the phone rang. His father was dying of a heart attack.

Brian flew to the hospital. The whole flight, he was filled with hope. Finally they could talk. Finally he could hear what he'd waited his whole life to hear.

But when he walked in, his father was in a coma. Had been for hours. Four hours later, his father died without waking up.

Brian stood there saying the same words over and over: "Dad, please wake up. Please wake up."

He wasn't just mourning the loss of his father. He was mourning the loss of any chance at his father's blessing.

---

We're all like Brian. We long for a blessing. We want the people in our lives — parents, spouses, children, friends, co-workers — to tell us that we matter to them.

In the Old Testament, Esau couldn't wait to receive the family blessing from his father Isaac. But his brother Jacob stole it. You remember the story — Isaac sent Esau out to bring back a meal before the blessing. While Esau was hunting, Jacob disguised himself and went to his nearly blind father pretending to be Esau. And Isaac blessed him.

When Esau came back:

> "My father, sit up and eat some of my game, so that you may give me your blessing." His father Isaac asked him, "Who are you?" "I am your son," he answered, "your firstborn, Esau." Isaac trembled violently and said, "Who was it, then, that hunted game and brought it to me? I ate it just before you came and I blessed him — and indeed he will be blessed!" When Esau heard his father's words, he burst out with a loud and bitter cry and said to his father, "Bless me too, my father!" — Genesis 27:31-34 CSB

That cry — "Bless me too" — is the same cry you can hear, though often silently, from thousands of people who have never received a blessing.

But here's the thing. Even if you haven't received one, and even if you haven't been giving one — it's not too late to start.

Three elements of a blessing. If you apply them, they will change your life and the lives of the people around you.

---

## I. Provide a Meaningful Touch

When Isaac blessed his son, he said, "Come near now and kiss me, my son." Every time a blessing was given in Scripture, there was hugging, kissing, or the laying on of hands. Meaningful touch communicates warmth, acceptance, and affirmation.

You know the story of the little four-year-old girl frightened during a thunderstorm. After a loud clap of thunder she ran to her parents' room. Her father said, "Don't worry, honey. The Lord will protect you." She snuggled closer and said, "I know that, Daddy, but right now I need someone with skin on."

God knows we need that. The comfort and security that comes through human touch.

Look at Jesus. He touched people constantly — children and grown men alike. In Mark 10, he called the little children to his side, took them in his arms, put his hands on them, and blessed them. On another occasion, he reached out and touched a man with leprosy. Healed him.

People are starving for affection. A hug. A hand on the shoulder. A handshake. These things can make a real difference in someone's life.

---

## II. Offer Spoken Words of Encouragement

"Sticks and stones may break my bones, but words will never hurt me."

That's a lie. Words do hurt. They can destroy a friendship and rip apart a home.

> With the tongue we praise our Lord and Father, and with it we curse men, who have been made in God's likeness. — James 3:9 CSB

Our tongues were created to bless, not curse. To build up, not tear down. When we praise, encourage, and affirm people — we are blessing them.

In the Old Testament, Abraham spoke a blessing to Isaac. Isaac spoke it to Jacob. Jacob spoke it to each of his twelve sons and two of his grandchildren. A blessing in Scripture is not a blessing unless it is spoken out loud.

Have you blessed someone lately with kind words? Have you told your spouse how much you love them? Told your kids or grandkids how proud you are? Thanked a co-worker?

One kind word can change a life.

When I was in eighth grade, I tried out for the basketball team. I had many challenges to overcome. I was short. I was skinny. My hair was too long.

But I had a coach who blessed me. Coach Weiner showed me how to play the game. He told me I was going to be a fine point guard. He always encouraged me.

Not long ago I saw him at a convenience store and he told me something I never knew. A parent had come to him and said, "Keith Smith will never be a basketball player. He will never amount to anything." Coach Weiner told her she was wrong.

Eventually I grew a little. Cut my hair. During my senior year at Duncanville, I was awarded most valuable player in our district. I received a scholarship to play at Dallas Baptist University.

That happened because of a coach's blessing.

---

## III. Demonstrate Commitment

A meaningful touch and words of encouragement are important. But the mortar that holds them together is active commitment — a willingness to see the blessing come to pass.

> If a brother or sister is naked and destitute of daily food, and one of you says to them, "Depart in peace, be warmed and filled," but you do not give them the things which are needed for the body, what does it profit? — James 2:15-16 CSB

Words without action are useless. A blessing is not complete without a commitment to follow through.

Coach Weiner didn't just tell me I could play. He taught me how to shoot, how to pass, how to lead on the court. He committed his time and effort to make the blessing real. Commitment takes work. You have to invest energy to see a blessing become a reality in someone's life.

The final chapter of Proverbs describes a woman who blessed her family in every way — industrious, loving, committed to her husband and children. She wasn't born that way. Each quality was developed at a price. She was up at dawn. She worked hard.

Was it worth it? Listen to what her family said:

> Her children rise up and call her blessed; her husband also, and he praises her: "Many daughters have done well, but you excel them all." — Proverbs 31:28-29 CSB

It takes hard work to bless another person. It takes time to hug your children when they come home from school. It takes courage to speak encouraging words. It takes effort to help someone achieve their blessing.

But one day — maybe years later — that blessing comes back. Your children and grandchildren will rise up and bless you.

---

God wants us to bless others. Romans 12:14 says so plainly.

Provide a meaningful touch. Speak words of encouragement. Commit to seeing the blessing through.

Go bless someone this week.
